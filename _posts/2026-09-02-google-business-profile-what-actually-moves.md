For a business that serves a geographic area, the Google Business Profile is usually worth more than the website. It's also where the worst advice on the internet lives, a genre of listicle in which every claim is a percentage with no study behind it.

So this post is built from two sources only: **Google's own documentation**, and the **Whitespark 2026 Local Search Ranking Factors** report, which surveys local SEO practitioners and is the closest thing the field has to a shared evidence base. Where they disagree, I'll say so.

## What Google says, in Google's words

Google's own guidance on local ranking is short enough to read in five minutes, and it says this:

> "Local results are mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity."

Relevance is how well your profile matches the search. Distance is how far you are from the searcher. Prominence is how well-known the business is, and Google is explicit that **"more reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking."**

Google also says, plainly: **"There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google."** Anyone selling you guaranteed map-pack placement is selling you something Google says does not exist.

And: **"Businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results."** Which is the least exciting and most reliable advice in local SEO, fill it in, all of it, correctly.

## What practitioners measured for 2026

Whitespark's 2026 report ranks the factors its respondents believe drive local pack and Maps results. Their top five for the local pack:

1. **Primary business category**
2. **Proximity of your address to the searcher**
3. **Keywords in the business title**
4. **Physical address in the city being searched**
5. **Being open at the time of the search**

For local *organic* results, the blue links under the map, the top factors look completely different, and this is the finding most small businesses miss:

1. **A dedicated page for each service you offer**
2. **Geographic relevance of your page content**
3. **Quality and authority of links to your domain**

Three practical readings of that list.

**Your primary category is the single highest-leverage field in the entire profile.** One dropdown. If you're a plumber whose primary category is "Contractor," you are competing in the wrong race. Pick the most specific category that describes your main service, and use secondary categories for the rest.

**Hours are a ranking factor, not just a courtesy.** "Business is open at the time of search" sitting in the top five means stale holiday hours cost you visibility, not just goodwill.

**One page per service.** A single "Services" page listing eleven things loses to eleven pages. This is the highest-value website work most local businesses never do, and it's the thing you should do *instead of* obsessing over the profile once the profile is complete.

## Three rules that get profiles suspended

This part matters more than any optimization, because a suspended profile is worth zero regardless of your category choice.

**1. Your business name must be your real-world business name.** Not "Plumber Dallas 24/7 Emergency Plumbing." Google's naming guidelines require the name you actually use in the real world, on your signage and paperwork. Keyword stuffing the title is an effective tactic right up until it isn't, and the penalty is your listing.

**2. Never gate reviews.** Google's prohibited content policy is explicit that businesses must not "discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers." Routing happy customers to Google and unhappy ones to a private form is exactly that. Ask everyone, the same way, every time, and don't pressure people on the premises either, which the same policy also covers.

**3. Photos should be your actual business.** Google's photo and video policy requires imagery that genuinely represents the business and complies with its content policies. I'd add a practical rule on top: don't post a generated image of a storefront, van or team you don't have. Even where a policy line is arguable, a customer who arrives to a place that looks nothing like the photos has already decided something about you.

## One myth worth killing

`LocalBusiness` schema markup on your website is not a documented map-pack ranking lever. Google's structured data documentation describes what the markup does, it tells Google about hours, departments and the like, and can change how your business *appears* in results, and Google explicitly does not guarantee that features consuming structured data will show at all. Meanwhile Google's own local ranking guidance never mentions markup: it lists relevance, distance and prominence. Google has also said structured data isn't required for its AI features. Add it because it's tidy and cheap. Don't add it expecting to move up the map pack.

## A one-hour checklist

If you do nothing else this month:

- [ ] Primary category is the most specific accurate option
- [ ] Every service you sell is listed in the Services section, with descriptions
- [ ] Hours are correct, including holiday hours for the next three months
- [ ] Business name matches your real-world name exactly, no keywords
- [ ] At least a dozen real photos, taken by you, within the last year
- [ ] Every review from the last year has a reply
- [ ] A standing, unfiltered process for asking every customer for a review
- [ ] Your website has a real page for each service, not one page listing them all

That list will beat almost any agency retainer sold on the promise of a ranking Google says can't be bought.
