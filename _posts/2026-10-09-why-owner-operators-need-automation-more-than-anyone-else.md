Lisa owns a three-chair salon in Tacoma. She cuts hair Tuesday through Saturday, answers the phone between clients, sends appointment reminders after closing, reconciles the books on Sunday afternoon, and orders color on Monday mornings. She is good at what she does. The Yelp reviews say so. But she has not had time to return a lead inquiry in three days, and her kid's parent-teacher conference is in twenty minutes.

She is not unusual.

Small business owners work an average of [52 hours each week, with 62% working 50 or more hours](https://news.gallup.com/poll/18088/work-labor-love-smallbusiness-owners.aspx), according to the Wells Fargo/Gallup Small Business Index. And a [2023 survey of 251 US entrepreneurs](https://www.timeetc.com/resources/how-to-achieve-more/the-big-price-of-small-tasks-how-entrepreneurs-may-be-unwittingly-keeping-their-businesses-small/) reported that small administrative tasks occupied 36% of an average 45.5-hour workweek, commissioned by virtual assistant company Time etc.

That is roughly 16 hours a week spent on work that does not require your specific skill. Scheduling. Data entry. Sending invoices. Logging expenses. Chasing down a receipt. Tasks that are necessary, repetitive, and in the way of everything else you are trying to build.

## The problem is not the hours

Owner operators do not lack work ethic. Most would happily put in fifty hours if those hours went to the work they started the business to do. The frustration comes from what the hours are made of.

A [survey of 1,000 American small business owners](https://www.scrippsnews.com/business/small-business-owners-wear-5-hats-daily-but-many-say-the-creative-ones-fit-the-worst) found that the average owner wears five hats on any given day, according to research commissioned by Adobe Express and conducted by Talker Research. They are simultaneously playing customer service representative, marketer, bookkeeper, social media manager and creative director. With all of those hats, the average entrepreneur is working over 200 bonus hours annually.

When you are the only one in the building, or one of two, every hat lands on your head. You are the scheduler when someone calls. You are the bookkeeper when QuickBooks needs attention. You are customer service when a client has a question at 7 p.m. You are marketing when you remember you have not posted anything in a week.

And somewhere in all of that, you are supposed to do the skilled work that earns the revenue.

## What repetitive work actually costs

A [2023 report from Ramsey Solutions](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/business/small-business-labor-crisis) found that 42% of small business owners say they have experienced burnout in the past year.

It is not hard to see why. When your day is spent toggling between the work you trained for and the fifteen other things no one trains you for, nothing gets your full attention. A plumber who spends two hours a day on scheduling, invoicing and supply orders is a plumber who does fewer jobs. A consultant who spends a third of her week on admin is a consultant whose pipeline dries up because she has not had time to follow up with leads.

The cost is not just in the hours. It is in what does not happen because the hours are full of the wrong work.

Lisa, the salon owner, is not struggling because she lacks clients. She is struggling because the rhythm of her week does not leave room to bring in the next ones. A lead calls on Tuesday afternoon while she is mid-cut. She makes a note. By evening she has forgotten. By Thursday the lead has booked somewhere else.

That is not a marketing problem. It is a bottleneck problem, and the bottleneck is her.

## The argument for automation is not about technology

Automation is not about replacing you. It is about getting repetitive decisions off your desk so you can do the work only you can do.

When someone books online instead of calling, that is one fewer interruption during the workday. When appointment reminders go out without you having to remember to send them, that is fifteen minutes back. When a lead gets an immediate text confirmation instead of waiting two days for you to call back, that is a customer you did not lose to someone faster.

Growing SMBs are [1.6 times as likely as their stagnant or declining counterparts](https://a.sfdcstatic.com/content/dam/www/ocms/assets/pdf/smb/Small-Medium-Business-Trends_Salesforce-Research.pdf) to say they are using technology to automate business processes, according to Salesforce Research.

The businesses that grow are not the ones working longer hours. They are the ones who stopped doing work that does not need them.

## The caveat

This does not apply evenly. If you run a business where the customer experience depends on every interaction being personal and your volume is low enough that you can maintain that without strain, automation may solve a problem you do not have. A freelance writer with six long-term clients does not need a CRM. A photographer who books ten weddings a year may not need online scheduling.

But if you are turning away work because you are too busy, if leads are slipping through because you did not follow up in time, or if you are working nights and weekends just to keep the lights on, the bottleneck is not your capacity. It is what your capacity is being spent on.

## What a fix involves

Fixing this means deciding which parts of your week do not need to be manual anymore, then making them stop being manual. You see where the repetitive work lives, what falls through when you are busy, and what a customer would never know you forgot. Then you route those tasks somewhere else: online booking instead of phone tag, text confirmations instead of handwritten reminders, an intake form instead of twenty questions asked out loud.

The goal is not to automate everything. It is to get the work that does not need you out of your way so you can do the work that does.
